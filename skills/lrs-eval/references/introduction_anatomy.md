# Introduction Anatomy — McEnerney's 4-Part Framework

Source: Larry McEnerney, "The Craft of Writing Effectively" (UChicago Leadership Lab lecture) and "The Problem of the Problem" handout (distributed at UChicago, Emory, OSU, Reed College).

---

## The Four Parts

Every effective academic introduction moves through these four stages in order:

### 1. Stasis (Shared Common Ground)

**What it is**: A statement of what the reader community currently accepts as true. This is the stable ground — the world as readers understand it before the paper disturbs it.

**Purpose**: Establish rapport with the community. Show you understand what they believe. Build from their knowledge, not yours.

**Signal words**: "It is well established that...", "Research consistently shows...", "Scholars generally agree...", "The standard approach to..."

**Length**: Typically 1-3 sentences. Brevity signals confidence; over-long stasis suggests the writer is filling space.

**Common errors**:
- Too much stasis → reads as a textbook summary, not an argument
- Stasis that nobody disputes → wastes the reader's time
- Stasis about the writer's topic rather than the reader's beliefs → fails to connect

**Example (epidemiology)**:
"Sleep duration is a well-established predictor of cardiometabolic risk, with meta-analyses consistently reporting elevated hazard ratios for adults sleeping fewer than 6 hours per night (Cappuccio et al., 2010; Itani et al., 2017)."

### 2. Destabilizing Condition

**What it is**: The moment the paper introduces instability — something that challenges, complicates, contradicts, or undermines the stasis. This is the engine of the paper. Without destabilization, there is no reason to read.

**Purpose**: Create intellectual tension. Make the reader's current understanding feel inadequate. Generate the need for new knowledge.

**Signal words**: however, but, although, yet, nonetheless, nevertheless, on the other hand, surprisingly, paradoxically, despite this, in contrast, unfortunately, problematically, critically.

**Types of destabilization** (from strongest to weakest):
1. **Contradiction**: "However, recent evidence directly contradicts this..."
2. **Inconsistency**: "Yet findings across populations are inconsistent..."
3. **Error**: "This assumption contains a systematic error..."
4. **Complication**: "Although true in general, this breaks down when..."
5. **Limitation**: "But these findings are limited to..."
6. **Gap** (weakest): "However, no study has examined..." — avoid if possible

**Length**: 1-3 sentences. The destabilization should be sharp and specific.

**Common errors**:
- Using "gap" language when instability language is available → low value
- Burying the destabilization in the middle of a long paragraph → reader misses it
- Multiple destabilizations → dilutes the tension; pick the strongest one

**Example**:
"However, virtually all evidence linking short sleep to cardiometabolic risk derives from urban populations with access to sleep medicine services. Rural communities — where shift work, caregiving demands, and provider scarcity make standard sleep recommendations impractical — are absent from this evidence base."

### 3. Cost / Consequence

**What it is**: The price the reader community pays for not resolving the destabilization. This is what makes the paper urgent. Without cost, the instability is merely interesting; with cost, it demands action.

**Purpose**: Make the reader feel the problem personally. The cost must be to THEM — their patients, their policies, their research programs, their clinical decisions — not to "science" or "the field" in the abstract.

**The McEnerney test**: Can a specific reader in the target community finish the sentence "Because of this problem, I am currently..." doing something wrong, making a poor decision, wasting resources, or harming someone?

**Signal words**: "As a result...", "Consequently...", "This means that...", "Without this evidence...", "Clinicians/policymakers/researchers therefore..."

**Length**: 1-2 sentences. Be specific and concrete.

**Common errors**:
- Abstract costs ("further research is needed") → no urgency
- Writer-centric costs ("we don't know enough") → reader doesn't care about your knowledge
- Costs to "the field" instead of to specific people → fails the McEnerney test
- No cost at all → most common failure; writers assume the gap speaks for itself

**Example**:
"Without evidence from these populations, clinicians serving rural Appalachia apply sleep guidelines developed for and validated in urban settings — potentially recommending interventions that are inaccessible, culturally mismatched, or physiologically inappropriate for their patients."

### 4. Resolution / Promise

**What it is**: What the paper will deliver to resolve the instability and eliminate the cost. This is the promise the paper makes to readers.

**Purpose**: Give readers a reason to continue reading. The resolution should directly address the destabilization and reduce the cost.

**Signal words**: "In this study, we...", "To address this...", "Here, we...", "This paper...", "We therefore..."

**Length**: 1-3 sentences. Should clearly state what was done and what it will yield.

**Common errors**:
- Resolution that doesn't match the destabilization → reader feels betrayed
- Over-promising ("we definitively resolve...") → sets up disappointment
- Under-specifying ("we contribute to this literature") → vague, no commitment

**Example**:
"We therefore examined sleep duration, timing, and regularity in a population-based cohort of 1,200 adults from rural eastern Kentucky, testing whether cardiometabolic associations observed in urban samples replicate in a community where structural barriers to sleep health are pervasive."

## The Complete Introduction (assembled)

> Sleep duration is a well-established predictor of cardiometabolic risk, with meta-analyses consistently reporting elevated hazard ratios for adults sleeping fewer than 6 hours per night. **[STASIS]**
>
> However, virtually all evidence linking short sleep to cardiometabolic risk derives from urban populations with access to sleep medicine services. Rural communities — where shift work, caregiving demands, and provider scarcity make standard sleep recommendations impractical — are absent from this evidence base. **[DESTABILIZATION]**
>
> Without evidence from these populations, clinicians serving rural Appalachia apply sleep guidelines developed for and validated in urban settings — potentially recommending interventions that are inaccessible or physiologically inappropriate for their patients. **[COST]**
>
> We therefore examined sleep duration, timing, and regularity in a population-based cohort of 1,200 adults from rural eastern Kentucky, testing whether cardiometabolic associations observed in urban samples replicate in a community where structural barriers to sleep health are pervasive. **[RESOLUTION]**

## Diagnostic Rubric

| Component | STRONG | ADEQUATE | WEAK | ABSENT |
|-----------|--------|----------|------|--------|
| Stasis | Specific, concise, connects to reader beliefs | Present but generic | Too long or not connected to destabilization | No shared ground established |
| Destabilization | Sharp contradiction/inconsistency with signal words | Present but uses gap language | Buried or vague | No tension; jumps to "we studied X" |
| Cost | Specific, concrete, to identifiable reader community | Present but generic ("public health implications") | Abstract or writer-centric | Missing entirely |
| Resolution | Directly addresses destabilization; specific method and scope | Present but vague | Over-promises or doesn't match destabilization | Missing or "this study addresses this gap" |

## Quick Reference: Destabilization Signal Words

**Strong** (mark contradiction): however, but, yet, although, nevertheless, nonetheless, in contrast, on the contrary, paradoxically, surprisingly, contrary to expectations

**Medium** (mark complication): while, whereas, despite, even though, notwithstanding, at the same time, on the other hand

**Weak** (mark gap): few studies, little is known, no study has examined, remains unexplored, has received limited attention — AVOID when instability language is available
