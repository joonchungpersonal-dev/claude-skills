# LRS 21 Principles — Central Reference

Synthesized from Joseph Williams (sentence clarity), Gregory Colomb (argument architecture), George Gopen (reader expectation approach), and Larry McEnerney (value to readers, problem construction). Source material: Williams "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace," Booth/Colomb/Williams "The Craft of Research," Gopen & Swan "The Science of Scientific Writing" (American Scientist 1990), McEnerney "Craft of Writing Effectively" lecture, McEnerney "Problem of the Problem" handout.

---

## Tier 1 — Value & Argument (McEnerney/Colomb)

### P01: Value to Readers
**Source**: McEnerney
**Definition**: Writing must be valuable to a specific reader community, not merely "clear" or "organized." Value is defined by the community, not the writer. Readers read to acquire knowledge that matters to their work.
**Diagnostic**: Does the paper articulate why readers should care? Is the payoff framed in terms of what readers gain, not what the writer did?
**STRONG**: Opening paragraphs explicitly state what readers will gain; value proposition is community-specific.
**ADEQUATE**: Value is implied but not foregrounded; readers can infer relevance.
**WEAK**: Paper assumes inherent interest; no explicit value proposition.
**ABSENT**: Paper reads as a report to oneself; no reader orientation.
**Example**:
- Before: "This study examined the relationship between sleep duration and cognitive decline."
- After: "Clinicians treating older adults lack evidence on whether habitual short sleep accelerates cognitive decline — a gap that delays screening recommendations."

### P02: Problem Construction
**Source**: McEnerney
**Definition**: The introduction must establish instability or tension — something the reader community should find problematic. Without a problem, there is no reason to read. The structure is: Stasis → Destabilizing Condition → Cost → Resolution.
**Diagnostic**: Does the introduction move from stable common ground to a destabilizing condition? Is the cost articulated in terms meaningful to readers?
**STRONG**: Clear stasis, sharp destabilization with signal words (however, but, yet), explicit cost to reader community, and a promise of resolution.
**ADEQUATE**: Problem is present but the cost is vague or writer-centric.
**WEAK**: Introduction presents background and a "gap" but no instability.
**ABSENT**: Introduction is a literature summary with no tension.
**Example**:
- Before: "Few studies have examined sleep quality in rural Appalachian populations. This study addresses this gap."
- After: "Sleep interventions developed for urban populations assume access to sleep clinics and CBT-I providers. However, rural Appalachian communities lack both — meaning current clinical guidelines may be inapplicable to 60 million Americans."

### P03: Community Codes
**Source**: McEnerney
**Definition**: Every disciplinary community has "code words" that signal value and instability. Using these words tells readers the paper matters. Missing them means the paper fails to register as important.
**Diagnostic**: Does the paper deploy discipline-specific value words? Do these words appear in the introduction and discussion?
**STRONG**: Key code words from the target community appear in title, abstract, introduction, and discussion. They signal instability (inconsistent, contradictory, problematic, overlooked, undermines).
**ADEQUATE**: Some code words present but not in strategic positions.
**WEAK**: Generic academic language; no discipline-specific markers.
**ABSENT**: Paper could belong to any field; no community signaling.

### P04: Instability over Gaps
**Source**: McEnerney
**Definition**: Frame the motivation as an error, inconsistency, or contradiction in existing knowledge (instability = high value) rather than merely "no one has studied X" (gap = low value). Gaps are boring; instabilities are urgent.
**Diagnostic**: Is the motivation framed as "we don't know X" (gap) or "we think X but evidence suggests not-X" (instability)?
**STRONG**: Explicit instability — current knowledge is wrong, contradictory, or has unrecognized costs.
**ADEQUATE**: Partial instability — some tension but falls back on gap language.
**WEAK**: Pure gap framing — "little is known," "no study has examined."
**ABSENT**: No motivation at all; jumps to methods.
**Example**:
- Gap (weak): "No study has examined sleep regularity in Appalachian adults."
- Instability (strong): "National sleep guidelines assume regularity improves health outcomes, yet data from non-urban populations — where work schedules and caregiving demands make regularity impractical — suggest this assumption may be wrong."

### P05: Argument Structure
**Source**: Colomb (Booth, Colomb & Williams "The Craft of Research")
**Definition**: Every argument requires: Claim → Reasons → Evidence → Acknowledgment & Response → Warrant. Missing components weaken the persuasive chain.
**Diagnostic**: Can each major claim be decomposed into these five components? Are warrants explicit or assumed?
**STRONG**: All five components present for major claims. Acknowledgment & Response handles obvious objections. Warrants are stated or clearly implied.
**ADEQUATE**: Claim-Reason-Evidence present but A&R or Warrant missing.
**WEAK**: Claims asserted with evidence but no reasoning chain.
**ABSENT**: Claims without evidence or reasoning.

### P06: Changing Reader Ideas
**Source**: McEnerney
**Definition**: The function of writing is to change readers' ideas, not to communicate yours. If readers' ideas don't change, the writing has failed regardless of how clear it is.
**Diagnostic**: What idea does the reader hold before reading? What idea should they hold after? Is the delta explicit?
**STRONG**: The paper explicitly identifies a reader's prior belief and demonstrates why it should change.
**ADEQUATE**: The paper adds information but doesn't explicitly frame the conceptual shift.
**WEAK**: The paper reports findings without connecting to what readers previously believed.
**ABSENT**: The paper is purely descriptive; no conceptual movement.

## Tier 2 — Sentence Clarity (Williams/Gopen)

### P07: Characters as Subjects
**Source**: Williams
**Definition**: The main characters (agents, actors) in your story should be the grammatical subjects of your sentences. When abstract nouns or nominalizations occupy the subject position, readers struggle to identify who is doing what.
**Diagnostic**: Are the grammatical subjects of sentences the actual agents performing actions?
**STRONG**: 80%+ of sentences have concrete agents as subjects.
**ADEQUATE**: 60-80% concrete agents; some abstract subjects.
**WEAK**: Frequent abstract subjects ("The analysis," "The investigation," "The utilization").
**ABSENT**: Pervasive agent-free prose.
**Example**:
- Before: "The investigation of sleep patterns revealed an association..."
- After: "We investigated sleep patterns and found an association..."

### P08: Actions as Verbs
**Source**: Williams
**Definition**: Express key actions as verbs, not as nominalizations (nouns derived from verbs). "Investigate" not "investigation," "analyze" not "analysis" (when used as the action). Nominalizations drain energy from prose and obscure who does what.
**Diagnostic**: What is the nominalization density? Are key actions expressed as verbs?
**STRONG**: Nominalization density < 3 per 100 words; actions consistently expressed as verbs.
**ADEQUATE**: Nominalization density 3-5 per 100 words; most key actions are verbs.
**WEAK**: Nominalization density 5-8 per 100 words; many buried actions.
**ABSENT**: Nominalization density > 8 per 100 words; prose is almost entirely nominal.
**Example**:
- Before: "The determination of the association between the variables was conducted through the application of regression analysis."
- After: "We determined how the variables were associated by applying regression analysis."

### P09: Subject-Verb Proximity
**Source**: Gopen
**Definition**: Keep the grammatical subject and its verb close together — ideally within 7 words. When long modifiers or qualifiers separate subject from verb, readers must hold the subject in working memory while processing the interruption.
**Diagnostic**: What is the average word count between subject and verb? How many sentences exceed 7 words of separation?
**STRONG**: Average S-V distance < 5 words; < 10% of sentences exceed 7.
**ADEQUATE**: Average S-V distance 5-7 words; 10-25% exceed 7.
**WEAK**: Average S-V distance 7-10 words; 25-50% exceed 7.
**ABSENT**: Routine S-V separations > 10 words.
**Example**:
- Before: "Participants who reported sleeping less than six hours per night and who had no prior diagnosis of insomnia or other sleep disorder were excluded."
- After: "We excluded participants who reported sleeping less than six hours per night and had no prior diagnosis of insomnia or other sleep disorder."

### P10: Topic Position
**Source**: Gopen
**Definition**: Begin sentences with old, known, or expected information (the "topic position"). This anchors readers in familiar ground before introducing new information. Sentences that begin with new information disorient readers.
**Diagnostic**: Do sentences begin with information the reader already knows from the previous sentence or section context?
**STRONG**: 80%+ of sentences begin with old/known information; transitions are smooth.
**ADEQUATE**: 60-80% begin with old information; occasional disorientation.
**WEAK**: Frequent new-information openings; reader must re-anchor repeatedly.
**ABSENT**: No discernible pattern; sentences begin with whatever comes first.

### P11: Stress Position
**Source**: Gopen
**Definition**: End sentences with new, important, or emphatic information (the "stress position"). Readers naturally emphasize what comes last. Placing trivial information at the end wastes the most powerful position in the sentence.
**Diagnostic**: Does the most important new information land at the end of each sentence?
**STRONG**: Key findings, new concepts, and emphatic points consistently end sentences.
**ADEQUATE**: Stress position used well in most sentences; some wasted.
**WEAK**: Important information buried mid-sentence; sentences trail off with qualifiers.
**ABSENT**: No strategic use of sentence endings.
**Example**:
- Before: "Cognitive decline was associated with short sleep duration in our adjusted models."
- After: "In our adjusted models, short sleep duration was associated with cognitive decline."

### P12: Given-New Contract
**Source**: Williams, Gopen
**Definition**: Across sentences, progress from given (known) information to new information. Each sentence should begin where the previous one left off (given) and end with something the reader hasn't seen yet (new). This creates a chain of understanding.
**Diagnostic**: Do sentence endings connect to the next sentence's beginning? Is there a discernible given-new chain?
**STRONG**: Clear given-new chains across most paragraph sequences; readers never lose the thread.
**ADEQUATE**: Given-new chains present in most sequences; occasional breaks.
**WEAK**: Frequent given-new breaks; reader must infer connections.
**ABSENT**: Sentences appear in random order; no discernible chain.

## Tier 3 — Paragraph Coherence (Williams)

### P13: Topic Strings
**Source**: Williams
**Definition**: Within a paragraph, the grammatical subjects of consecutive sentences should form a consistent "topic string." When subjects jump around (the study... the participants... the results... the analysis...), readers lose track of what the paragraph is about.
**Diagnostic**: List the subjects of each sentence in a paragraph. Do they form a consistent string?
**STRONG**: 80%+ of sentences in each paragraph share or closely relate to the same topic subject.
**ADEQUATE**: Topic strings are present but break 2-3 times per paragraph.
**WEAK**: Subjects shift every 1-2 sentences; no dominant topic.
**ABSENT**: Every sentence has a different subject; paragraph reads as a list.

### P14: Point Sentences
**Source**: Williams
**Definition**: Every paragraph needs a clear point sentence — usually the first or second sentence — that tells readers what the paragraph will demonstrate, claim, or argue. Without it, readers must read the entire paragraph to discover its purpose.
**Diagnostic**: Can you identify the point sentence in each paragraph? If you remove all other sentences, does the point sentence alone convey the paragraph's purpose?
**STRONG**: Every paragraph has a clear, identifiable point sentence, usually at the beginning.
**ADEQUATE**: Most paragraphs have point sentences; a few require reading through to find the point.
**WEAK**: Point sentences are buried or ambiguous in many paragraphs.
**ABSENT**: Paragraphs are collections of sentences with no discernible point.

### P15: Cohesion (Flow)
**Source**: Williams
**Definition**: Cohesion is the local connection between adjacent sentences — sentence endings should connect to the next sentence's beginning. Cohesive prose flows; incohesive prose jerks.
**Diagnostic**: Does the end of each sentence set up the beginning of the next? Can you draw a connecting thread?
**STRONG**: Smooth, unbroken flow between adjacent sentences throughout most paragraphs.
**ADEQUATE**: Flow breaks occasionally but recovers; reader can follow.
**WEAK**: Frequent flow breaks; reader must supply missing connections.
**ABSENT**: Sentences are isolated units; no inter-sentence connection.

### P16: Coherence (Whole)
**Source**: Williams
**Definition**: Coherence is the global unity of a paragraph — all sentences develop a single, unified topic that the point sentence announces. Cohesion is local glue; coherence is the overall architecture.
**Diagnostic**: Does every sentence in the paragraph contribute to the point sentence's claim? Are there digressions?
**STRONG**: Every sentence advances the paragraph's point; no digressions.
**ADEQUATE**: Most sentences are on-topic; 1-2 minor digressions.
**WEAK**: Multiple digressions or tangential sentences; point gets lost.
**ABSENT**: No unified topic; paragraph covers 3+ unrelated ideas.

### P17: Thematic Strings
**Source**: Williams
**Definition**: The information at the end of sentences (stress position) across a paragraph should form a coherent thematic progression — a "thematic string" that carries the paragraph's developing argument forward.
**Diagnostic**: List the end-of-sentence information for each sentence in a paragraph. Does it form a logical progression?
**STRONG**: End-of-sentence information builds progressively toward the paragraph's conclusion.
**ADEQUATE**: Thematic string present but includes some random or repetitive elements.
**WEAK**: End-of-sentence information is scattered; no progression.
**ABSENT**: No discernible thematic pattern.

## Tier 4 — Document Structure (McEnerney/Colomb)

### P18: Introduction as Problem
**Source**: McEnerney
**Definition**: The introduction must function as a problem statement, not as background. Its job is to create tension that the paper will resolve. Background is in service of the problem, never an end in itself.
**Diagnostic**: If you remove the background sentences, does the introduction still have a clear problem? Or does removing background remove everything?
**STRONG**: Introduction is structured as Stasis → Destabilize → Cost → Resolution (see introduction_anatomy.md). Background serves the problem.
**ADEQUATE**: Problem is present but buried in background; reader must extract it.
**WEAK**: Introduction is mostly background with a token problem sentence at the end.
**ABSENT**: Introduction is pure background or literature summary.

### P19: Lit Review as Enrichment
**Source**: McEnerney/Colomb
**Definition**: The literature review should highlight conflicts, contradictions, and unresolved tensions — not merely summarize what others have found. Each cited paper should advance the problem or establish the instability.
**Diagnostic**: Does each paragraph of the lit review contribute to the problem? Or is it a neutral summary?
**STRONG**: Each cited work is positioned relative to the paper's instability; conflicts and contradictions are highlighted.
**ADEQUATE**: Some strategic positioning; some neutral summaries.
**WEAK**: Chronological or alphabetical literature summary; no positioning.
**ABSENT**: No lit review, or lit review has no connection to the problem.

### P20: Reader-Centric Framing
**Source**: McEnerney
**Definition**: Frame every section for what readers need to understand, not for what the writer did. "We collected data from N participants" is writer-centric. "The sample comprised N adults, enabling detection of effect sizes as small as..." is reader-centric.
**Diagnostic**: Could you rewrite each section heading as "What readers need to know about [topic]"? Does the section deliver that?
**STRONG**: Sections are framed around reader needs; methods explain why choices matter to readers.
**ADEQUATE**: Mixed framing; some reader-centric, some writer-centric.
**WEAK**: Predominantly writer-centric ("We did X, then Y, then Z").
**ABSENT**: Pure chronological report of writer's activities.
**Example**:
- Writer-centric: "We used actigraphy to measure sleep."
- Reader-centric: "Sleep was measured by actigraphy (7 consecutive days), providing objective estimates free from self-report bias."

### P21: Costs Borne by Readers
**Source**: McEnerney
**Definition**: Frame consequences in terms of costs to the reader community — not abstract costs to "the field" or "science." Who specifically is harmed by not knowing this? What decisions are they making poorly?
**Diagnostic**: Are the consequences specific to a reader community? Can a reader in that community feel the cost?
**STRONG**: Costs are specific, concrete, and attributable to identifiable reader communities.
**ADEQUATE**: Costs are present but generic ("public health implications").
**WEAK**: Costs are abstract or tacked on ("further research is needed").
**ABSENT**: No articulation of consequences.
**Example**:
- Abstract: "This has implications for public health policy."
- Reader-centric: "Without this evidence, geriatricians will continue prescribing sleep hygiene protocols designed for working-age adults — protocols that may worsen nocturia-related insomnia in their patients."
